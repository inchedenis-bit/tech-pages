The KES 25,000 Question: Is a Refurbished Laptop Actually Safer Than a Cheap New One?

Editorial September 7, 2026 Comparisons

You have KES 25,000 in your pocket. You walk into a shop, and the seller shows you two options. On the left is a brand-new laptop, still sealed in its box. On the right is a refurbished laptop that looks a bit heavy and has a sticker from a foreign company on it.

The new one feels safer. But the used one will probably work better.

This is the hardest choice in the Kenyan laptop market. We are taught to fear second-hand things because we worry they will break. But when it comes to computers, the truth is often reversed.

Here is a simple breakdown of why a refurbished laptop is usually the safer bet, and when you should walk away.

The Problem With the "New" Laptop

A brand new laptop for KES 25,000 sounds like a great deal. But to get the price that low, the manufacturer had to use the cheapest parts possible.

These laptops usually have a Celeron processor and only 4GB of RAM. They are built for basic web browsing, nothing more. If you open more than three tabs in Chrome, the laptop will freeze. If you try to run Zoom, it will sound like an airplane taking off. Within six months, you will be frustrated.

The biggest danger is that these cheap new laptops usually have a mechanical hard drive (HDD) instead of a solid-state drive (SSD). An HDD is an old technology that is painfully slow. It is like trying to drive a car with the handbrake on.

The "Refurbished" Reality

Now, look at the refurbished laptop on the right.

A refurbished laptop is usually a machine that was bought by a big company in Europe or America for their staff. They used it for two or three years, wiped it clean, and sold it in bulk to dealers who ship them to Kenya.

For KES 25,000, you can buy an older HP EliteBook or Lenovo ThinkPad. Yes, it is heavier. Yes, it might have a small scratch on the lid. But inside, it is a completely different machine.

It has a strong processor (like an 8th-gen Intel Core i5). It has an SSD, which means it turns on in five seconds. It is built out of metal, not cheap plastic. It is designed to survive being thrown into a bag every day.

The Only Thing That Actually Matters

If you are buying a laptop on a budget, there is one golden rule: You are better off with an old, fast laptop than a new, slow one.

The refurbished business laptop will let you type documents, join online classes, and browse the internet without making you angry. The cheap new laptop will look shiny for a week, and then it will become a source of stress.

The Catch: How to Buy It Safely

A refurbished laptop is safer only if you buy it the right way. Because they are imported in bulk, some sellers try to hide problems.

Here is what you must do before you hand over your KES 25,000:

  1. Ask for the Generation: Do not just ask for an "i5." Ask what generation it is. An 8th-gen i5 is good. A 4th-gen i5 is too old.

  2. Check the Battery: Ask the seller how long the battery lasts. If it dies in one hour, you will need to buy a replacement, which costs extra.

  3. Get a Warranty: Buy from a seller who offers a six-month warranty. If they say "no returns," walk away.

The Verdict

If you have exactly KES 25,000, do not buy a new laptop. The specs on those cheap new machines are a trap.

Instead, buy a refurbished business laptop. Get one with an SSD and at least an 8th-generation processor. It might not look brand new, but it will do everything you need it to do, and it will still be working long after the cheap new laptop has been thrown in a drawer.
